Vivaldi's Op. 10, published in Amsterdam around 1729, was the first set of concertos for transverse flute ever printed. Before that the instrument was a curiosity in the orchestra; after that it had a repertoire. Six concertos, and the second one is *La notte* — the night.

It is a strange piece, structurally. Instead of the usual fast–slow–fast, it runs through a sequence of short movements with titles: a *Largo* opening, then *Fantasmi* — ghosts — then a second *Largo*, a *Presto*, then *Il sonno*, sleep, and finally an *Allegro*.

Vivaldi is not being coy about any of it. *Fantasmi* is the whole orchestra lunging in jagged unison at something that isn't there, breaking off, lunging again. *Il sonno* is the extraordinary one: the strings sink to a murmur, the harmony stops moving altogether, and the flute is left breathing over the top of it. Four bars of held chords going nowhere — the sound of a room at three in the morning.

Vivaldi wrote roughly five hundred concertos and is regularly accused of writing the same one five hundred times. *La notte* is the answer to that charge.
